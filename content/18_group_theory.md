# Introductory Group Theory

Group theory is the mathematics of symmetry, and quantum computing is built
from a small number of groups: the Pauli group, which describes errors; the
Clifford group, which describes the gates that can be simulated efficiently
classically; and the stabiliser groups that define quantum error-correcting
codes. This lesson introduces the four group axioms using the Pauli matrices
you already know, then works through the Pauli and Clifford groups concretely.
It is classified as a *recommended* prerequisite rather than a required one,
because the error-correction material motivates it rather than depending on it
line by line.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- **State** the four group axioms and test whether a given set with an
  operation satisfies them.
- **Identify** generators of a group, and construct a Cayley table for a small
  group.
- **Recognise** the Pauli group and state its order, and compute products such
  as $XY = iZ$.
- **Explain** what the Clifford group is and why conjugating a Pauli by a
  Clifford element returns another Pauli.
- **Describe** how stabiliser groups are used to define quantum error-correcting
  codes.

## The group axioms

### Intuition

A group is a set of reversible actions that can be composed. "Do nothing" must
be one of the actions, every action must be undoable, and composing two
actions must give another action in the same set.

### Formal definition

A **group** $(G, \cdot)$ is a set $G$ with a binary operation satisfying:

1. **Closure**: for all $a, b \in G$, $a \cdot b \in G$.
2. **Associativity**: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
3. **Identity**: there exists $e \in G$ with $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$.
4. **Inverses**: for each $a \in G$ there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ with $a \cdot a^{-1} = e$.

Commutativity is *not* required. A group where $ab = ba$ always holds is
called **abelian**.

### Worked example: the Pauli matrices are not a group

$\{X, Y, Z\}$ with matrix multiplication fails closure: $XY = iZ$, which is not
in the set. Add the identity and the phases $\{\pm 1, \pm i\}$ and it becomes
one — that is the Pauli group below.

### Generators and order

A subset $S \subseteq G$ **generates** $G$ if every element of $G$ can be
written as a product of elements of $S$ and their inverses. The **order** of a
group is its size; the **order** of an element $g$ is the smallest $k > 0$ with
$g^k = e$.

A **Cayley table** is the multiplication table of the group. It makes closure
and the identity visible at a glance.

## The Pauli group

### Definition

The **Pauli group on one qubit** $P_1$ is the set of all products of the Pauli
matrices with the phases $\{\pm 1, \pm i\}$:

$$X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

There are 4 Pauli matrices ($I, X, Y, Z$) times 4 phases, giving

$$|P_1| = 16$$

### The multiplication table

The products, computed by direct matrix multiplication, are:

| | $X$ | $Y$ | $Z$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $X$ | $I$ | $iZ$ | $-iY$ |
| $Y$ | $-iZ$ | $I$ | $iX$ |
| $Z$ | $iY$ | $-iX$ | $I$ |

Three facts follow immediately:

- Each non-identity Pauli **squares to the identity**: $X^2 = Y^2 = Z^2 = I$, so each has order 2.
- Distinct Paulis **anticommute**: $XY = iZ$ while $YX = -iZ$, so $\{X, Y\} \equiv XY + YX = 0$. The same holds for $\{Y, Z\}$ and $\{X, Z\}$.
- Products close **up to a phase**, which is exactly why the phases must be included for the set to be a group.

The group is generated by $X$ and $Z$ together with the phases, since
$Y = iXZ$ up to a sign.

### Physical meaning

In error correction, the Pauli group is used because **any** error on a qubit
can be expressed as a linear combination of $I, X, Y, Z$. Correcting those
four cases corrects every possible single-qubit error. This is the
discretisation of errors that makes quantum error correction possible at all,
and it is revisited in the stabiliser formalism lesson.

## The Clifford group

### Definition

The **Clifford group** is the set of unitary operators that map Pauli
operators to Pauli operators under conjugation:

$$\mathcal{C} = \{U \text{ unitary} : U P U^\dagger \in P_n \text{ for every } P \in P_n\}$$

It is the **normaliser** of the Pauli group.

### Conjugation examples

With $H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ and
$S = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}$, direct computation gives:

$$H X H^\dagger = Z, \qquad H Z H^\dagger = X$$

$$S X S^\dagger = Y, \qquad S Z S^\dagger = Z$$

Each result is again a Pauli, confirming that $H$ and $S$ are Clifford
operators. Together they generate the Clifford group (up to global phase),
which is why $\{H, S, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ is such a common gate set.

### Why the Clifford group matters

The Gottesman–Knill theorem states that circuits built only from Clifford
gates, computational-basis preparation and measurement can be simulated
efficiently on a classical computer. So Clifford circuits are **not**
universal for quantum computing — a non-Clifford gate such as $T$ is needed to
reach the full power of quantum computation. This is the dividing line behind
the distinction between "easily simulable" and "genuinely quantum", and it is
why the $T$ gate is counted separately in resource estimation.

## Stabiliser groups

### Intuition

Instead of listing the states a code uses, a **stabiliser code** specifies the
operators that leave those states unchanged. The code space is the simultaneous
$+1$ eigenspace of a chosen abelian subgroup of the Pauli group.

### Formal sketch

Let $\mathcal{S}$ be an abelian subgroup of $P_n$ that does not contain $-I$.
The **code space** is

$$\mathcal{H}_{\text{code}} = \{|\psi\rangle : M|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle \text{ for every } M \in \mathcal{S}\}$$

An error $E$ is detected by measuring the generators of $\mathcal{S}$: if $E$
commutes with every generator the state is unchanged, and if it anticommutes
with some generator the measurement returns $-1$ and reveals a **syndrome**
identifying the error.

This is the conceptual core of the stabiliser formalism and surface codes that
appear later in the curriculum. You do not need it to follow those lessons,
but it explains why they are structured the way they are.

## Practical example

```python
import numpy as np

I = np.eye(2, dtype=complex)
X = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]], dtype=complex)
Y = np.array([[0, -1j], [1j, 0]], dtype=complex)
Z = np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]], dtype=complex)
H = (1 / np.sqrt(2)) * np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]], dtype=complex)
S = np.array([[1, 0], [0, 1j]], dtype=complex)
PAULIS = {"I": I, "X": X, "Y": Y, "Z": Z}


def name(M):
    """Express a 2x2 matrix as a phased Pauli, or '?' if it is not one."""
    for label, P in PAULIS.items():
        for phase, prefix in ((1, ""), (-1, "-"), (1j, "i"), (-1j, "-i")):
            if np.allclose(M, phase * P):
                return f"{prefix}{label}"
    return "?"


# --- the Pauli multiplication table ----------------------------------------
print("Pauli products:")
for a in "XYZ":
    for b in "XYZ":
        print(f"  {a}{b} = {name(PAULIS[a] @ PAULIS[b])}")

# --- squaring and anticommutation ------------------------------------------
print("\nsquares:", {n: name(P @ P) for n, P in PAULIS.items()})
for a, b in (("X", "Y"), ("Y", "Z"), ("X", "Z")):
    A, B = PAULIS[a], PAULIS[b]
    print(f"  {{{a},{b}}} = 0 (anticommute)? {np.allclose(A @ B, -(B @ A))}")

# --- the full Pauli group has 16 elements ----------------------------------
elements = []
for label in "IXYZ":
    for phase in (1, -1, 1j, -1j):
        M = phase * PAULIS[label]
        if not any(np.allclose(M, e) for e in elements):
            elements.append(M)
print(f"\n|P_1| = {len(elements)}")
print("closed under multiplication:",
      all(any(np.allclose(A @ B, C) for C in elements)
          for A in elements for B in elements))

# --- Clifford conjugation maps Paulis to Paulis ----------------------------
print("\nClifford conjugation:")
for gname, G in (("H", H), ("S", S)):
    for pname in "XYZ":
        print(f"  {gname} {pname} {gname}^dag = "
              f"{name(G @ PAULIS[pname] @ G.conj().T)}")

# --- element orders ---------------------------------------------------------
for n, P in PAULIS.items():
    order = next(k for k in range(1, 9) if np.allclose(np.linalg.matrix_power(P, k), I))
    print(f"order({n}) = {order}")
```

Running it confirms the table above exactly: `XY = iZ`, `XZ = -iY`,
`YX = -iZ`, all three anticommutators vanish, $|P_1| = 16$ with closure
holding, and the four Clifford conjugations return `Z`, `X`, `Y`, `Z`. Every
non-identity Pauli has order 2.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A group must be commutative."** Most interesting groups are not. The Pauli
  group is non-abelian: $XY \neq YX$.
- **"$\{X, Y, Z\}$ is the Pauli group."** It is not closed. The phases
  $\{\pm 1, \pm i\}$ are essential members.
- **"Clifford circuits give quantum speed-up."** By the Gottesman–Knill
  theorem they are classically simulable. A non-Clifford gate such as $T$ is
  required for universality.
- **"Group theory is only abstract algebra with no computational use."** The
  stabiliser formalism — the basis of most practical quantum error correction —
  is entirely group-theoretic.
- **"The order of a group is the same as the order of its elements."** The
  group's order is its size; an element's order is the power that returns it to
  the identity.

3. Show that $X$ and $Z$ together with the phases generate all 16 elements of

6. Explain why a subgroup used for stabilisers must not contain $-I$.

## Summary

- A group satisfies closure, associativity, identity and inverses;
  commutativity is optional.
- The Pauli group $P_1$ has 16 elements: the four Pauli matrices times the
  phases $\{\pm 1, \pm i\}$. Distinct Paulis anticommute and each squares to
  $I$.
- The Clifford group is the normaliser of the Pauli group: $U P U^\dagger$ is
  always a Pauli. It is generated by $H$, $S$ and CNOT.
- Clifford-only circuits are classically simulable (Gottesman–Knill), so a
  non-Clifford gate is needed for universal quantum computation.
- Stabiliser codes define a code space as the simultaneous $+1$ eigenspace of
  an abelian Pauli subgroup, and detect errors via syndrome measurements.
